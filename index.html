Welcome

Start here.

I’m Nate Langley, a studio art major at the University of Maryland, College Park, and a future art teacher. I’m in UMD’s Integrated Master’s Certification Program, so I’ll finish my B.A. in Studio Art in 2027 and my Master of Education in 2028. Outside the studio I’m the graphics director at WMUC 90.5 FM, where I also host a weekly radio show, and I work with kids as an art teacher and childcare aide.

I made this portfolio to show what I make and how I think, for anyone deciding whether to hire me, show my work or hand me a classroom.

Inside are three artifacts: two sculptures and a painting. All three ask the same question from a different direction: what does control look like? One looks at the pop culture we consume, one at who gets to frame a story, and one at the people we put in power. Each has its own page with photos and a statement on what the piece is, why I made it and what it shows about how I work. Use the menu to get around. The artifacts are listed in order, Other works holds my writing, and About and resume has my resume, my radio show and my contact info. Click any photo to see it full size.

The three artifacts

Poo Man’s Love

Artifact 1 of 3. Mixed-media sculpture.

MediumWood, deconstructed troll doll, VHS reel and tape (from Ice Age), acrylic paint
SizeAbout 14 in tall, 11 in wide
Made2026

Contextual statement

Poo Man’s Love is a mixed-media sculpture about 14 inches tall and 11 inches wide. The coiled body is wood, shaped and painted with acrylic. The face and arms come from a troll doll I took apart, and the halo is a reel from a VHS copy of Ice Age, with a strip of the tape still hanging off it. I made it in October 2026 for ARTT333 Elements of Sculpture: Wood and Mixed Media with Foon Sham.

I kinda just jumped straight into this one without much thought. I figured it would be funny to make, so I made it. In the end, I think of it as sort of a false angel: a serpent with a toy’s grin, arms open for a hug, wearing a halo made from a kids’ movie. It also works like an idol, something to set on a table and look up to, and it got me thinking about how much we let culture shape us and how quickly that culture turns over. A troll doll and a VHS tape were both everywhere once. Now they’re thrift-store leftovers, and something else has taken their place. If the things we consume get that much say in who we are, is there a real difference between them and a false idol? The more I think about that question the more it interests me, and this sculpture is me asking it with a straight face.

What this piece shows about how I work is that I use humor on purpose. A joke gets people close enough to an object that the harder question can reach them, and I’d rather someone laugh first and think second than walk past. It also shows how I handle materials. I can shape wood into a form that reads clearly from across a room, take found objects apart and give them a new job, and use paint to make mismatched parts read as one figure. Choosing an Ice Age tape over a blank one is the kind of small decision I care about, because the specifics are where the meaning and the joke both live.

Untitled (The Monolith)

Artifact 2 of 3. Welded steel sculpture.

MediumWelded steel
Size6 ft tall, exactly my height
Made2025
NowRusting in the woods at an undisclosed location

Contextual statement

Untitled (The Monolith) is a welded steel sculpture that stands six feet tall, which is exactly my height. Holes cut near the top line up at eye level, and two arched slots at the base leave room for your feet. I made it in 2025 for ARTT331 Elements of Sculpture: Steel with Jeremy Kunkel. After it was shown, I left it in the woods in a place I’m not disclosing, where it will rust and decay on its own schedule.

I also coated it in my own rusting agent concoction to kickstart the process. In the first photo at the top of the page, you can see the beginning of the rust after I initially sprayed it. As the months passed, the spots that rusted the most and turned orange fastest were the spots I had sprayed. I’m very pleased with how it turned out.

The piece is about controlled narratives. The slots at the bottom invite you to step right up to it, and once you’re there the holes work as a viewfinder: you see what it’s pointed at and nothing else. I choose which way it faces, so I choose what you see. The part that interests me most is where that control runs out. This thing will likely outlive me. I’ve abandoned it, and whoever finds it won’t get a wall label or an artist statement. To them it has no title at all. I call it The Monolith after the one in 2001: A Space Odyssey, which I kept thinking about while I built it: an object that changes whoever finds it and explains nothing. So do I decide what it means, or do the people who use it? What will you call it?

As evidence of what I can do, this piece shows I can plan and fabricate at full human scale: measuring a design to a body, cutting and welding steel, and making an object that stands on its own outdoors. It shows I design for time, since the rust is part of the work and not damage to it. It also shows how I think about an audience. The height, the eye-level holes, the foot slots and even the title are each a decision about what a viewer will physically do, and that habit of designing for someone else’s experience is one I’ll carry into teaching.

Pet

Artifact 3 of 3. Painting.

MediumAcrylic and colored pencil on cardboard (the bottom of an Arizona tea box)
SizeApprox. 8 in × 5 in
Made2025

Exhibition statement

The central figure of the piece stands as a symbol of paternalistic influence and forceful suppression. His hand sensually yet firmly envelops the head of the other man beneath him. The three blue stars, often a symbol of national pride, here feel heavy and intrusive, suggesting that the ‘patriotism’ this figure is performing is inextricably linked to the control and exploitation of the vulnerable. What are the true intentions of the people we elect into power? What does their authority hide?

The statement I wrote for the 2026 Sadat Arts for Justice & Peace Competition.

Contextual statement

Pet is a small painting in acrylic and colored pencil on cardboard. The cardboard is the bottom of an Arizona tea box. I showed it at the Herman Maril Gallery from March 9 to April 3, 2026, with the exhibition statement above. This one was not made for a class. It served as a much needed release for me at the time.

Almost everything in the painting is red, blue or black. The stripes in the background and the three stars on the hoodie put a flag into the scene without my having to paint one. The larger figure is too big for the frame and gets cropped by its edges, while the smaller one fits completely under his hand. That hand is where the title comes from: it’s how you’d pat a pet. It reads as affection and as ownership in the same gesture, and I wanted both readings to stay open because that is how paternalistic power usually presents itself.

This piece shows that I can carry a political idea through composition, color and gesture instead of spelling it out. It shows I can work small and cheap. I painted the flat areas in acrylic and drew the faces and hair in colored pencil, so the people are the most fragile, hand-drawn things in the picture, and the surface is something most people throw away. It also shows I can write about my own work for a public audience, which the exhibition statement required.

About and resume

Who I am, what I’ve done and how to reach me.

I’m an artist and art teacher in training based in College Park, Maryland. I work in sculpture, painting, illustration and graphic design, and I write. I’m drawn to things that are loud, strange, or make me uncomfortable because I believe the best art comes from curiosity rather than playing it safe.

Off the clock I collect and read comics (Hellboy is my favorite series), paint Warhammer miniatures, and DJ a weekly radio show.
